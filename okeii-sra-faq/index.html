The basics

6 questions
What Is the Strong Readers Act?

The Strong Readers Act establishes a statewide framework for improving reading instruction and intervention in Oklahoma public schools.

The law establishes expectations for:

  • Early identification of reading difficulties.
  • Explicit and systematic reading instruction.
  • A Multi-Tiered System of Supports (MTSS).
  • Individualized reading intervention.
  • Progress monitoring.
  • Parent communication and involvement.
  • Teacher training and literacy support.
  • Additional intervention opportunities.
  • Reading proficiency expectations for third grade.

The Legislature states its intent that every student read at or above grade level by the end of third grade.

What Does This Mean for My Child?

The Strong Readers Act is designed to identify reading difficulties before they become larger academic problems.

If a student is identified as having a reading deficiency, the school can provide increasingly intensive levels of support based on the student’s needs and response to instruction.

A reading deficiency does not mean a child is a failure. It means the school has identified an area where additional support may be needed.

The goal is to provide that support as early as possible.

How Will Reading Be Taught?

The Act calls for explicit and systematic instruction in foundational reading and writing skills.

These include:

  • Phonological awareness
  • Decoding
  • Encoding
  • Spelling
  • Sentence-level writing
  • Language development
  • Fluency
  • Vocabulary
  • Comprehension

The law also requires kindergarten through third-grade reading instruction to incorporate the five elements of reading instruction—phonological awareness, decoding, fluency, vocabulary and comprehension—using high-quality instructional materials grounded in the science of reading and selected from materials approved by the State Board of Education.

What Is the Science of Reading?

The Science of Reading is not one particular curriculum or textbook.

It refers to the body of research that helps explain how children learn to read and the instructional practices that support reading development.

Under the Strong Readers Act, Oklahoma emphasizes explicit and systematic instruction in foundational reading skills, along with vocabulary, fluency and comprehension.

What About Three-Cueing, Balanced Literacy and Whole Language?

The Act prohibits Oklahoma school districts and charter schools from using the three-cueing system model of teaching students to read, which directs students to identify or guess words in their context.

The law defines three-cueing as an approach based on meaning, structure, syntax and visual cues. It also identifies terms including MSV, balanced literacy and whole language in connection with this model.

The purpose is to emphasize systematic instruction in the skills students need to decode and understand written language.

What Is MTSS?

MTSS stands for Multi-Tiered System of Supports.

It is a framework that provides strong core literacy instruction to all students and increasingly intensive instructional support based on student need and demonstrated response to instruction.

In simple terms:

  • All students receive strong core literacy instruction.
  • Students who need additional help receive targeted intervention.
  • Students who need more intensive support receive intensive, or small-group, intervention.

Reading support

6 questions
What Happens If My Child Struggles With Reading?

Students identified with reading deficiencies may receive increasingly intensive intervention based on their needs and response to instruction.

Reading intervention plans are designed to identify the student’s needs, provide supplemental instructional services and monitor progress.

The intervention process includes monitoring the student’s reading progress and adjusting instruction according to the student’s needs.

The goal is not simply to provide more instruction. It is to determine whether the instruction is helping the student make meaningful progress.

Will My Child Miss Math to Receive Reading Intervention?

No.

The Strong Readers Act prohibits required reading intervention from being provided during math instruction.

The intent is to strengthen reading without sacrificing instruction in another core academic area.

Will Reading Intervention Be Done on a Computer?

Technology may be used as a supplemental resource, but the law does not allow reading intervention to be provided solely through digital technology.

A majority of reading intervention must consist of direct instruction from a teacher or reading specialist trained in the Science of Reading.

Who Develops My Child’s Reading Intervention Plan?

The Student Reading Proficiency Team develops the reading intervention plan.

The team includes:

  • The student’s parent or legal guardian.
  • The teacher responsible for the student’s reading instruction.
  • A teacher responsible for reading instruction in the student’s next grade.
  • A certified reading specialist or interventionist, early literacy micro-credentialed personnel or another appropriately trained literacy professional, when available.
  • The principal, vice principal or designee.

This structure is intended to bring the people most directly involved with the student together to develop and monitor support.

What Role Do Parents Have?

Parents are an important part of the reading intervention process.

Parents are members of the Student Reading Proficiency Team when an individual reading intervention plan is developed.

Parents should have the opportunity to understand:

  • Their child’s reading needs.
  • The instruction being provided.
  • The intervention being used.
  • Their child’s progress.
  • Additional support that may be appropriate.

The law also requires notification when a student in kindergarten through third grade is identified as having a reading deficiency.

What If My Child Is Not Making Progress?

Progress monitoring is an important part of the Strong Readers approach.

Schools are required to maintain documentation showing:

  • How the student was identified.
  • The intervention approach being used.
  • The frequency, duration and group size of intervention.
  • Ongoing progress monitoring.
  • How progress information is used to adjust instruction.

This creates a cycle:

Identify → Intervene → Monitor → Adjust → Continue Supporting

Grade by grade

5 questions
What Happens in First and Second Grade?

The Strong Readers Act places increased emphasis on addressing reading difficulties before students reach third grade.

A student not meeting reading targets triggers an immediate sequence of parental alerts and academic interventions, including:

  • Transitional instructional settings.
  • Before-, during- or after-school tutoring.
  • Summer reading programs.
  • Other approved reading interventions.

These interventions are intended to provide immediate academic support and involve parent communication.

The purpose is to give students additional opportunities to build reading skills before they reach the third-grade proficiency requirement.

What Is Summer Reading Intervention?

Summer academies and other approved programs are intended to help students successfully complete grade-level reading competencies and become better prepared for the next grade.

Summer reading programs are required to provide explicit, systematic, sequential and cumulative reading intervention addressing identified reading deficiencies.

For students who cannot attend a summer academy, districts may approve other options.

Why Is Third Grade Important?

Third grade is an important point in a student’s reading development.

By third grade, students must be able to decode and comprehend grade-level texts. Third grade is widely recognized by researchers, economists and educators as a critical watershed year because it marks the transition from learning to read to reading to learn.

The Oklahoma Center for Education Policy’s policy brief notes that third grade is a critical focus because students must be able to decode and comprehend grade-level texts.

What Does the Third-Grade Requirement Mean?

Beginning with the 2027-2028 school year, third-grade students must demonstrate sufficient reading skills for promotion.

The law establishes methods for demonstrating reading proficiency and provides specific provisions governing students who do not initially demonstrate sufficient reading skills.

Importantly, the Strong Readers Act is broader than a single test. It includes early screening, intervention, progress monitoring and parent involvement before a student reaches the third-grade decision point.

Is Third-Grade Retention Automatic After One Test?

No.

The Strong Readers Act establishes a process for determining whether a third-grade student has demonstrated sufficient reading skills.

The law provides methods for demonstrating proficiency and includes provisions addressing students who do not initially demonstrate sufficient reading skills. It also establishes good-cause exemptions and other statutory provisions related to promotion and retention.

The broader purpose is to ensure that students have the reading skills necessary to succeed in the next grade.

The research

4 questions
What Does Research Say About Third-Grade Retention?

Third-grade retention has been one of the most debated elements of early-literacy policy.

Mississippi’s literacy data shows that third-grade retention, when paired with intensive intervention, has been associated with significant and sustained reading gains, including gains that persist into middle school. The media talking points also note that retention itself accounts for only a small portion of overall academic growth, with systemic teacher training and early interventions aligned with the Science of Reading described as the larger drivers.

The Oklahoma Center for Education Policy’s January 2026 policy brief reviewed contemporary research on third-grade retention. Since Oklahoma follows Mississippi’s model, Mississippi’s research and experience are frequently discussed in connection with Oklahoma’s approach.

Earlier studies from the 1980s and 1990s often reported neutral or negative results. However, many of those studies did not adequately account for selection bias—the fact that students identified for retention may differ substantially from students who are not identified for retention.

More recent research uses stronger quasi-experimental methods, including comparisons of students near retention thresholds.

The OCEP review concludes that this newer body of evidence suggests third-grade retention can provide struggling third-grade students an opportunity to catch up academically.

What Does the Research Show?

The OCEP review identifies research from several states.

Michigan

A study involving 31,285 students found that students flagged for retention experienced increased reading scores the following school year, including students who were ultimately promoted.

Florida

Research found retained students slightly outperformed socially promoted students in reading during the first year following retention, with larger gains during the second year.

Indiana

Research found third-grade retention increased achievement in English language arts and mathematics, with effects persisting into middle school.

Mississippi

Research found substantial improvements in English language arts following third-grade retention, particularly among Black and Hispanic students.

The studies are discussed in the OCEP policy brief, which emphasizes that contemporary research provides stronger evidence than many of the older retention studies.

Does Research Prove Retention Is Always Beneficial?

No.

The research is more nuanced.

The evidence reviewed by OCEP generally points to academic benefits for struggling readers, but longer-term outcomes continue to be studied.

Retention should therefore be understood as one component of a broader literacy strategy—not as a stand-alone solution.

States that have adopted third-grade retention policies have generally combined retention with early screening, teacher training aligned with the Science of Reading and intensive early intervention.

What Can We Learn From Mississippi?

Mississippi is frequently cited in discussions of early-literacy policy.

The state implemented universal K–3 screening, individual reading plans and a third-grade retention measure.

When Mississippi’s literacy law took effect in 2013, the state ranked 49th nationally in fourth-grade reading on NAEP. By 2024, Mississippi ranked ninth.

Mississippi’s experience should be understood as an example of a comprehensive literacy strategy.

Third-grade retention was implemented alongside early screening, teacher training and intensive intervention.

Working together

4 questions
How Are Teachers Being Supported?

The Act includes provisions for teacher training and literacy expertise.

Each public elementary school is required to have access to at least one reading specialist, interventionist or teacher with an early literacy micro-credential.

The law also establishes teacher training academies focused on MTSS, the Science of Reading and early literacy instruction.

The intent is to provide schools with personnel who have specialized knowledge in evidence-based literacy instruction.

What Is a District Strong Readers Plan?

Each school district must adopt and implement a district Strong Readers plan.

The plan must include input from school administrators, teachers and parents or legal guardians and must outline how each school site will comply with the Strong Readers Act.

This provides districts with a framework for implementing the law while addressing the needs of individual schools.

What Should Parents Ask Their Child’s Teacher?

Parents can begin with five simple questions:

  1. How is my child progressing in reading?
  2. Is my child meeting grade-level reading targets?
  3. If my child is struggling, what specific skills need improvement?
  4. What intervention or additional support is being provided?
  5. How will we know whether the intervention is working?

Parents can also ask how they can support reading at home.

What Should Teachers Tell Parents?

Communication should focus on information parents can understand and use.

Parents should know:

  • Where their child is performing.
  • What reading skills need additional support.
  • What instruction or intervention is being provided.
  • How progress is being measured.
  • What the next steps will be.

Schools must notify parents when a student in kindergarten through third grade is identified as having a reading deficiency. The notification includes information about the student’s reading level, reading instruction and potential need for additional support.

The goal

What Is the Ultimate Goal?

The Strong Readers Act is not ultimately about a test, a retention number or a particular instructional program.

It is about helping children become successful readers.

The central approach is:

  • Identify the need.
  • Provide the right support.
  • Monitor progress.
  • Keep parents involved.
  • Give students the opportunity to succeed.

The objective is to help Oklahoma children develop the reading skills they need for success throughout their education. The central message to families is that reading proficiency by the end of third grade is a vital milestone that schools and parents must actively partner to achieve together.

Strong Readers. Stronger Futures.

The Strong Readers Act represents a statewide effort to strengthen early literacy by combining strong classroom instruction, early identification, targeted intervention, progress monitoring and parent involvement.

The Strong Readers Act requires transparency through parent notifications and gives families a formal role in developing individualized intervention plans and supporting learning at home before the third-grade proficiency requirement.

For families, the most important message is simple:

If a child is struggling to read, the earlier the problem is identified and addressed, the greater the opportunity to provide meaningful support.
